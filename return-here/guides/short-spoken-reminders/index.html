Short phrases to say to yourself when you need a pause.

Having the right few words ready is easier than finding them in the moment.

The short answer

A useful phrase is short enough to remember, true enough to believe, and aimed at what you need this minute: to slow down, to think again, or to start.

Decide which of those you need first. Then choose the words.

Three jobs a phrase can do

A phrase that helps on a frantic afternoon can be useless when you’re stuck on the sofa avoiding something. They are different problems. Return Here sorts its fifty phrases into three collections for this reason, and the same split works for words you write yourself.

01 / To slow downIn the app: Calm

When everything feels urgent at once.

These lower the stakes of the next minute. They don’t argue with the facts. They change the time frame.

  • This is one moment, not the whole day.
  • Nothing has to be solved in the next minute.
  • I can do this slowly.
  • One thing, then the next thing.
02 / To think againIn the app: Think

When you’re sure, and maybe shouldn’t be.

These are questions rather than statements. Use one before you send the email, or when the same argument has been running in your head for an hour.

  • What am I assuming here?
  • Is this urgent, or only loud?
  • What would I say to a friend in this spot?
  • What do I actually know so far?
03 / To get movingIn the app: Move

When you’ve paused long enough.

Plain and a little blunt. They shrink the task until starting it is hard to refuse.

  • Only the first five minutes.
  • Start badly. Fix it later.
  • Do the next small thing.
  • It doesn’t need to be good yet.

What makes a phrase work

These are the tests we use. They are editorial judgment, not science.

One breath long
If you can’t say it in a single breath, you won’t remember it when you need it. Aim for under a dozen words.
Plainly true
“Everything is fine” fails on a day when it isn’t. “I can handle the next hour” is easier to believe.
About now
The next step or the next minute. Not who you’ll be in five years.
No orders to feel
“Relax” and “be positive” tend to start an argument. A phrase can point your attention somewhere. It can’t command a mood.
In ordinary words
If it sounds like a poster, your mind will skim it like one.

Why hear it instead of read it

We have no study to cite here. These are practical observations.

  • A voice takes its own time. You can skim a sentence in half a second. A spoken one lasts as long as it lasts.
  • Your eyes are free. You can look out of the window instead of at another screen.
  • It isn’t your own inner voice. On a hard day that voice is often the one doing the damage, and the same words from someone else can be easier to take.

If reading works better for you, read. A sticky note is a perfectly good tool.

Ways to keep one close

  • Write it where the trouble happens. On the monitor, inside a notebook cover, or on a note by your desk.
  • Record yourself. A ten-second voice memo, saved where you can find it. It’s free, and they’re your own words.
  • Ask someone who knows you. A line from a friend who has seen you get through worse carries more weight than anything from a list.
  • Change it when it wears out. Any phrase goes quiet after enough repetitions. That’s normal. Pick another.

Words have limits. They can give you a moment of room on an ordinary hard day. They aren’t treatment, and if hard days have become most days, please talk to a professional.
