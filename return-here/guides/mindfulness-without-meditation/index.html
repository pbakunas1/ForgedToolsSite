Mindfulness, without the meditation session.

For people who like the idea of being present, and don’t want a cushion, a timer, or a streak.

The short answer

Yes, you can. Pick something you already do every day, like the first sip of coffee, and for those few seconds notice what it is actually like. When your mind wanders off, bring it back.

That coming back is the practice. Meditation is one way to train it, not the only way.

Meditation and mindfulness aren’t the same thing

Jon Kabat-Zinn, who brought mindfulness into mainstream medicine in the late 1970s, describes it as paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, without judging what you find. Nothing in that description mentions sitting still.

Teachers usually split practice in two. Formal practice is setting time aside: sitting, following the breath, ten or twenty minutes. Informal practice is bringing the same attention to something you were going to do anyway. Think of formal practice as the gym and informal practice as taking the stairs.

If you have tried meditation and it didn’t stick, that tells you about your relationship with the gym. The stairs are still there.

Seven moments you already have

The trick is to attach attention to an existing habit rather than add a new one. Choose one of these. Not all seven.

The first sip
The weight of the cup, the heat, the taste before your mind starts on the day’s list. After the first sip, drink the rest however you like.
Washing your hands
Water temperature, the smell of the soap, one hand moving over the other. Twenty seconds you were going to spend anyway.
One doorway
Pick a door you walk through often. Each time, feel your feet for two steps.
Waiting
The kettle, the elevator, a loading bar. Leave your phone where it is and count three sounds.
The walk to the car
Your pace, the air on your face, what the light is doing today.
The first minute of a conversation
Only listen. Notice the pull to plan your reply, and let the other person finish.
Brushing your teeth
Two minutes when your hands are busy and your mind usually isn’t in the room.

How to tell it’s working

Mostly, nothing special happens. You notice that the water is warm. Then you notice you’ve been thinking about an email for the last ten seconds, and you come back to the water. That’s a complete, successful round.

  • You don’t need to empty your mind. Minds produce thoughts. The skill is noticing that you left.
  • You don’t need to feel calm. You can be mindfully irritated. Noticing “I’m irritated” counts.
  • You don’t need to do it for long. A few seconds, done often, is the whole idea.
  • You can’t do it badly. Wandering off isn’t a mistake. It gives you something to come back from.

The hard part is remembering

Nobody finds this difficult to do. People find it difficult to remember. By the time you think of it, the coffee is finished.

  • One anchor for one week. Same moment every day until it starts to happen on its own. Then add a second if you want one.
  • Put a mark where the habit lives. A dot of tape on the kettle or a note on the bathroom mirror. It works until you stop seeing it, then move it.
  • Use a cue you choose. Something you start yourself, when you want it, tends to last longer than a notification that goes off while you’re busy and trains you to swipe it away.

What we won’t promise

Most research on mindfulness studies structured programs that run for weeks and include regular formal practice. Much less is known about a few seconds of attention at the sink, so we’re not going to tell you it lowers stress or changes your brain.

What we can say is smaller. It costs nothing and takes no extra time. On the days you do it, you will have been there for slightly more of your own day. Whether that’s worth it is yours to judge.
