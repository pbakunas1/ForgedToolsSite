A quick mental reset, in about a minute.

For when you’ve been interrupted, switched tasks too many times, or just read the same line three times.

The short answer

Stop. Write one line about where you left off. Take three slow breaths. Look at something farther away than your screen. Then name the one thing you’ll do next.

It takes about a minute. The first and last steps help you mark the transition.

The one-minute version

You don’t need a quiet room, and nobody around you needs to know you’re doing it.

  1. Park it.

    Write one line: where you stopped, and what the next move would be. “Budget draft, row 40. Next: ask Dana about the March number.” On paper, in the document itself, anywhere you’ll see it again.

    About 15 seconds

  2. Breathe.

    Three slow breaths. Counting four in and four out gives your attention something simple to hold. Comfortable matters more than exact.

    About 25 seconds

  3. Look up.

    Rest your eyes on something across the room or out a window. Notice one sound you had tuned out.

    About 10 seconds

  4. Pick one.

    Say the single thing you’ll do next, out loud or in your head. One thing, small enough to begin right now. “Reply to Sam,” not “catch up on email.”

    About 10 seconds

Try step two here

Three breaths, paced for you.

Four seconds in, four seconds out, three times. Breathe at whatever depth is comfortable, and stop whenever you like.

Count it yourself: in for four, out for four, three times.

Why the first step matters

Most quick-reset advice starts with breathing. We’d start with the note.

Sophie Leroy, a researcher at the University of Washington Bothell, uses the term attention residue for what happens when you leave a task unfinished: part of your attention stays behind with it. In experiments she ran with Theresa Glomb, people who took under a minute to jot down where they were and how they’d pick the work back up did better on the task that had interrupted them than people who simply switched. The university’s summary is here, and the 2018 paper is in Organization Science.

Two honest limits. These were lab studies about performance on work tasks, not about how people felt. And nobody has tested this exact four-step sequence. We put the note first because it is the part with evidence behind it, and because breathing calmly while still holding an unfinished thought in your head is harder than it sounds.

Between tasks: the shorter version

If you aren’t frazzled and simply need to move from one thing to the next without dragging the last one along, three beats are enough.

Close.
Write the one-line note, then physically close the thing: the tab, the notebook, the door to the meeting room.
Breathe once.
One slow breath with your hands off the keyboard. This is the whole pause.
Open.
Name the next task before you touch it, so you start on purpose instead of drifting in.

Where it helps

  • Walking out of a meeting and into writing that needs your full attention.
  • After a difficult phone call, before you dial the next one.
  • Sitting in the driveway after work, before going inside.
  • Coming back to your desk after someone “just had a quick question.”

Keeping your momentum

A pause costs you momentum when it turns into something else. Keep it under a minute, and don’t open a feed or your inbox in the gap. Checking messages feels like a break but hands you three new things to think about.

When a reset is the wrong tool

  • You’ve been going for hours. You need an actual break: food, water, a walk, daylight. A minute won’t stand in for that.
  • The work itself is the problem. Too much of it, or no clear next step. A reset will make that easier to see. It won’t fix it.
  • It’s been more than a busy day. If you’ve felt overwhelmed or unable to concentrate for days or weeks, a one-minute technique isn’t the right size of answer. Talk to someone you trust or to a professional.
  • You’re driving or doing anything else that needs your eyes and hands. Wait until you’ve stopped.
